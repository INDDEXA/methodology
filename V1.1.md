# INDDEXA Research Methodology — Version 1.1 (Revised)
Public research methodology, version 1.1, revised: how INDDEXA defines questions, uses evidence, writes, reviews and updates the research it publishes.

**Prepared by INDDEXA Research Department**

### Research method

This is Version 1.1, revised edition, of the INDDEXA research methodology. It describes how we define questions, use evidence, write, review and update the research we publish. The method is documented so that a piece can be examined, challenged and, where the sources allow, repeated in outline by another researcher.

The method applies to research articles and reports published under the INDDEXA name. It does not claim to be an external industry standard, and it does not replace professional or legal standards that may apply to a source we cite. Revisions keep the same canonical page; a substantive upgrade is published at its own versioned URL. This page is the current public baseline. Related public rules are the [Editorial Policy](/editorial-policy/) and the [Corrections Policy](/corrections/).

### 1. Purpose and scope

INDDEXA publishes evidence-led research on industries, companies, markets and business models. A study should help a professional reader understand a defined problem: how a sector is organised, how a firm creates value, how a technology becomes a business, or how a market is changing.

The unit of value is not length, novelty of wording, or speed of publication. The unit of value is an **information increment**: a contribution, relative to a stated baseline of already available information, that is supported by evidence and by a method another person can follow.

We do not treat "information increment" as a mathematical information-gain score. Whether a contribution exists is a qualitative judgement, made against a documented baseline and against the tests below.

We look for four things when we judge whether a contribution is there: **what is new relative to the baseline, what evidence supports it, what reproducible method produced it, and what its scope and uncertainties are**. All four should be possible to state. If one of them cannot be stated, we do not claim an increment.

### 2. The research question

Every study starts with a question that can be answered, not with a keyword target and not with a headline in search of a topic.

A usable question names:

- the object of study (an industry, firm, market, technology or defined comparison);
- the relationship, change or mechanism under examination;
- the geographic, sectoral and time bounds;
- the definitions that will be used for the main terms.

If those elements cannot be stated, the work does not proceed as a research article. A wide theme is narrowed or split before evidence collection begins. Company announcements and news reports may suggest a question; they do not, by themselves, set the scope.

### 3. Baseline of existing information

Before we claim to have added something, we record what is already known. The baseline is a list of existing facts, official disclosures, datasets, established interpretations, unanswered questions and the risk of repeating work already done. Important baseline items are tied to a source that a third party can locate.

The baseline is not a claim that a fact has never appeared anywhere on the internet. It is a claim that we have set down the relevant public record against which our own work should be judged. The baseline is fixed when the study is commissioned, not written after the findings are in. A missing baseline is a research defect, not a writing flaw.

### 4. What counts as a contribution

A published research article should contain at least one increment that can be checked. We classify contributions by their main type. A piece may have one primary type and supporting types. We assign the primary type in the following order, so that categories do not overlap by convenience:

1. **New fact** — a fact established by documentary investigation, observation or other verifiable work that was not adequately established in the baseline.
2. **Data contribution** — a dataset, statistic or comparison produced by independent collection, cleaning or analysis, with definitions and calculations stated.
3. **Independent verification** — a designed check of a public claim, a reported figure, or a stated performance, including a negative result where that is what the check shows. A negative result is a legitimate outcome of verification.
4. **Cross-finding** — a proposition that relates already public items which had not been connected explicitly, where the connection is supported by evidence.
5. **Interpretation** — a reasoned explanation of established facts, with the reasoning and its limits in view.
6. **Knowledge organisation** — a structured, documented and maintainable arrangement of scattered or conflicting information (for example a consistent definition table or a versioned map of a value chain).

The following are not, by themselves, contributions: paraphrase or translation; stitching together existing articles or announcements; unsupported speculation; technical language without sources or method; putting already public numbers into a new chart without a new question; unverified model-generated statements; or a change only to wording, title, keywords or the page date.

A summary of existing material may be a legitimate internal baseline or a reader aid. It is not sufficient as a research article unless the work also creates a maintained standard, a verification, or another contribution of the kinds listed above. An original interpretation is publishable only when it is grounded in evidence and is not written as if it were a confirmed fact.

Different techniques can help surface an increment. They are examples of approach, not requirements, and using one does not guarantee that an increment exists. For example: re-examine a widely reported question with a different time window, region or source type; trace a repeatedly cited figure back to its origin and recalculate it; connect items that are each public but not yet linked into a supported new proposition; rebuild a comparable dataset under one consistent definition; test whether a widely assumed premise actually has positive support and report honestly what was found within the sources and scope examined; or track how a stated commitment was met or changed over time. Whichever technique is used, the contribution is judged by the six definitions above and by section 5, not by the technique itself.

### 5. How a study is carried out

Once the question and baseline are in place, the work follows five steps.

**Step 1 — Fix the question.** Restate the question in operational terms. If it cannot be investigated with available methods and sources, the scope is reduced, the work is paused, or the topic is closed.

**Step 2 — Complete the baseline.** Record the main existing sources and the gap the study will address.

**Step 3 — Design the method.** State, before conclusions are drafted, how new information will be produced: sources to be collected, comparisons to be made, calculations to be run, checks to be performed, and known limits. The method is not inferred after the findings are written. Where an approach from section 4 is useful, it is chosen and written into the research plan at this stage, not added after the conclusions are known.

**Step 4 — Collect evidence and keep a record.** We distinguish a lead that suggested a question from a source that supports a claim. For material evidence we keep, where we are permitted to do so, enough information to retrace it: the source, the date of the original, the date we accessed it, the passage or field relied on, and any limitation. A core conclusion must be traceable to this record.

**Step 5 — State the increment.** Each increment offered for publication sets out four things: a claim that can be checked, at least one source that actually supports that claim, a method or chain of reasoning that can be reviewed, and a statement of scope and uncertainty — the four elements described in section 1.

We may use one or more of the following methods, according to the question: documentary research in official and company filings; official statistics; cross-source checking; consistent historical or cross-section comparison; product or process checks where they can be done openly and honestly; event studies around a clearly dated change; and, where appropriate, on-the-record specialist comment. Methods vary in cost and in the strength of evidence they can support. We do not describe an exploratory technique as if it were a certified external protocol.

A study ends when the question has been answered to the standard of this method, or when it becomes clear that the evidence will not support a research article. A negative result, a pause, or a decision not to publish is a legitimate outcome.

### 6. Sources and grades of evidence

Primary materials have priority: official statistics, regulatory documents, original filings, and formal company disclosures. Credible secondary research and professionally edited reporting may be used, with their status made clear. If a primary source cannot be obtained, we record why, whether the secondary source cites the original, whether any independent check exists, and what bias that substitution may introduce. A high-impact conclusion that rests on a single secondary source is weakened or withheld.

We treat sources as leads or as evidence, and we say which role a source is playing. Republication of the same original does not multiply the number of independent sources.

For internal work we distinguish three practical grades, which are working labels rather than a public accreditation:

- original or first-hand materials (a filing, an official dataset, a complete interview record, a documented test);
- credible secondary professional sources;
- background or lead material, including unverified third-party statements.

A single primary source may be enough to show that an organisation issued a statement. It is usually not enough to show that the effects claimed in that statement have occurred.

Every published piece is expected to maintain a claim record for its material assertions: the claim, whether it is fact, data, inference or opinion, the supporting sources and locations, the assessment of support, any counter-evidence, and the level of confidence. The test is not whether a link exists, but whether the cited passage actually supports the sentence.

When we report a negative result or a finding of "not found", we limit the conclusion to the sources listed and the scope examined; we do not turn it into a claim that something does not exist. To avoid a narrow result built only from sources likely to agree with us, we also record the sources that should have been checked but were not, and why. The qualifying language used in the title, the summary, the key findings and the body is consistent: a body that says "not found within the sources and scope listed" cannot support a title that states an absolute conclusion.

Data work follows the same discipline. A figure is published with its definition, unit, currency, geography, time window, sample, source, access date and, where relevant, the cleaning rule and formula. We do not add or compare figures on inconsistent definitions, treat correlation as causation, choose a window only to dramatise a trend, present a forecast as an outturn, or treat a case example as if it were the market.

### 7. Facts, inference and uncertainty

Material claims are expressed so that a reader can see their status. In working papers we use explicit labels; in the published article we use plain language to the same effect:

- confirmed;
- partly confirmed;
- not yet verified;
- disputed or in conflict across sources;
- inference drawn from evidence, which is not the same as a fact;
- opinion.

A core conclusion that depends on a serious uncertainty is stated with that uncertainty in view. We do not present conjecture as settled fact.

### 8. What a published piece contains

Articles follow a stable structure so that the question, the method and the limits can be found without reading the piece as rhetoric. The usual elements are:

- a title that states the question or the central finding;
- key findings;
- the research question and scope;
- why the question matters;
- what is already known;
- the approach and the sources;
- findings and analysis;
- implications for industry or business, where they are supported;
- limitations and uncertainties;
- a conclusion;
- sources and references.

Shorter briefings, standard analyses and longer research reports use the same discipline at different depth. Type-specific outlines (for example a value-chain study, a business-model analysis, a market study, a commercialisation study, or a benchmark study) change the emphasis, not the requirement to show a question, a baseline, a method and a supported increment.

Authors, reviewers and research notes are shown only when they are real. Each article is expected to state its research window and, where data are used, the date to which the data refer.

### 9. Review, originality and publication

A draft is checked for facts and figures before independent review. The reviewer is not the author. The reviewer tests whether the evidence supports the claims, whether fact and inference are kept apart, and whether the wording overreaches.

Before a piece is put forward for publication we ask, as a minimum gate:

- is there at least one increment that meets the standard in sections 4 and 5;
- is that increment supported by evidence that actually bears on the claim;
- is the method or reasoning open to review;
- have we said what is new relative to the baseline;
- does any veto condition in the editorial policy apply (including fabricated sources, a serious factual error, an unsupported core conclusion, plagiarism, or an undisclosed relevant interest)?

A piece that fails this gate is not sent to publication scoring or published as a research article. Strength in presentation does not offset the absence of a contribution.

Originality is assessed by more than one person and from more than one angle, including the increment against the baseline, the independence and support of the evidence, and the risk that the text is a rewrite, a mosaic of existing work, or an unverified model output. A single opinion does not decide that assessment. The assessment is an internal condition of submission; it is not a public score and not a substitute for human approval.

Publication requires a human decision. Scheduled or later public appearance does not relax the same checks.

### 10. Assistance from AI

AI tools may be used, under human supervision, to help find public sources, organise material, draft, and point to gaps. They may not invent interviews, data, tests, citations or identities. A model output is not an external source. A model does not review its own draft, and it does not take the decision to publish. Any specific fact or figure in the published text must be traceable to evidence.

Used as a tool, AI may carry out a method and judgements designed by people; that is permitted. What matters is that people design the method, take responsibility for the conclusions, and can trace every fact back to its source. Real interviews, field tests, access to paid databases, sensitive commercial judgement and final approval are carried out by people.

### 11. Updates, versions and corrections

Changing the page date is not a contribution. A substantive update adds evidence, data, verification, a correction of substance, or a new supported interpretation.

A revision differs from an upgrade. This edition is a **revision**: it clarifies wording and adds method examples and public-facing explanation, but it adds no public section, changes no public commitment, and alters no criterion or value. It therefore keeps the Version 1.0 label and keeps the same canonical page. Only a later upgrade that adds a public section or changes the scope of the public commitment receives a new version number and its own canonical URL, with earlier versions kept available.

Substantive changes raise the content version, update the modified date, and are recorded in a visible note. Earlier versions are retained. The [Corrections Policy](/corrections/) describes how errors are reported and handled.

### 12. Limits of this version

Version 1.1 is the first complete public statement of a method we already use internally. Some internal working scores and trial thresholds are used by editors as provisional management tools; they are not published here as standards, and they will be reviewed in the light of completed studies. Product types, section scope and the six contribution types above are in force.

This revised edition of Version 1.0 does no more than clarify wording and add public-facing explanation: the four-element test for an information increment, techniques for finding increments as optional examples, negative results as a legitimate outcome with consistent qualifying language, evidence grades as working labels, the boundaries of AI assistance, and the difference in URL handling between a revision and an upgrade. It does not change the scope of the public commitment, add or remove a contribution type, or publish any internal score or threshold.

Where a later version supersedes this page, the new version will be published at its own canonical URL, and this version will remain available for reference at [V1.0](/methodology/V1.0/).
